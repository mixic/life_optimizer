## AREA 1 — Formal game theory of the third party / spoiler

### 1.1 The spoiler problem

**Stedman, Stephen John. "Spoiler Problems in Peace Processes." *International Security* 22(2), Fall 1997, pp. 5–53.**
- Claim: spoilers are "'leaders and parties who believe that peace emerging from negotiations threatens their power, worldview, and interests, and use violence to undermine attempts to achieve it'"; they are "the greatest danger to peace processes"; Stedman develops a typology (position — inside/outside the process; number — limited/greedy/total; locus) mapping to management strategies (departing, dominating, socializing, buying off, isolating, spoiling).
- Method: comparative case analysis of 15 peace processes.
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION + MEASUREMENT (case-based typology).
- URL: https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/spoiler-problems-peace-processes (abstract quoted verbatim) · https://www.mitpressjournals.org/doi/abs/10.1162/isec.22.2.5

**Nilsson, Desirée & Mimmi Söderberg Kovacs. "Revisiting an Elusive Concept: A Review of the Debate on Spoilers in Peace Processes." *International Studies Review* 13(4), 2011, pp. 606–626.**
- Claim: the spoiler concept as used post-Stedman is elastic; the literature disagrees over definition, unit, and whether "spoiler" is a category or a behaviour; the authors argue for treating spoiling as a strategy rather than an identity.
- Method: systematic review of the spoiler literature.
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION.
- URL: https://academic.oup.com/isr/article-abstract/13/4/606/1799140 · https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/839176ed89ab878a77d6a252c8639e0cf5add214 (full text not readable this session — Cambridge/OUP blocks automated access)

**Zahar, Marie-Joelle. "Reframing the Spoiler Debate in Peace Processes." In *Contemporary Peacemaking* (Darby & Mac Ginty, eds.), Palgrave.**
- Claim: reframes spoiling as a negotiation relationship rather than a pathology, and questions Stedman's assumption that spoilers are identifiable ex ante.
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION. (Located via secondary citation only; primary text not read — flag.)

### 1.2 Divide and conquer / divide and rule — formal literature

**Che, Yeon-Koo, Jinyuqi Huang & Wooyoung Lim. "First They Came for the Others: A Theory of Divide-and-Conquer." arXiv:2607.12371 [econ.TH].**
- Claim: divide-and-conquer succeeds through "epistemic friction regarding an aggressor's underlying intent" rather than mechanical coordination failure: bystanders must infer whether an attack is a localized grievance or a systemic campaign, and if it reads as particularized they abstain; "higher attack costs and lower correlation between victims' fates facilitate this division."
- Method: formal game-theoretic model of inference under incomplete information.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT — but a recent preprint, not peer-reviewed. This is the only genuine formal divide-and-conquer model located.
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12371

**Weingast, Barry R. "The Political Foundations of Democracy and the Rule of Law." *American Political Science Review* 91(2), 1997, pp. 245–263.**
- Claim: a sovereign's ability to transgress against one group depends on other groups' failure to coordinate a defence of the target; division of the opposition is what makes predation possible.
- Method: coordination-game formalisation with a sovereign and multiple citizen groups.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. Closest formal result to "a third party exploits division."
- DOI: 10.2307/2952354

**Chen, X. "A Theory of Divide-and-Rule: Kleptocracy and Its Breakdown." University of Warwick working paper.**
- Claim: divide-and-rule can arise in equilibrium because the ruler can shift resources to the proposing group.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT (working paper).
- URL: https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/intranet/manage/calendar/x_chen_a__theory_of_divide-and-rule.pdf (PDF not readable via tooling this session — snippet only)

**Brams, Steven J. & Alan D. Taylor. *Fair Division: From Cake-Cutting to Dispute Resolution.* Cambridge University Press, 1996.**
- Claim: envy-free and proportional division procedures, including divide-and-choose, with existence theorems for general n.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. **Caveat: divide-and-choose is fair division, not strategic coalition-splitting. Do not conflate.**
- URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/fair-division/...

**Riker, William H. *The Theory of Political Coalitions.* Yale University Press, 1962.**
- Claim (as attributed): the "size principle" — coalitions tend to minimum winning size.
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION, not a theorem. Exact wording not verified; the specific empirical refutation (Browne & Franklin, APSR) was not readable this session. **Do not cite a refutation effect size.**

**Empty categories (formally searched, nothing peer-reviewed found):** "divide et impera" in mechanism design; "salami tactics" formal models; formal models of a third party that profits from two others' discord; "strategy of tension" as a formal model; formal models of *manufacturing* a security dilemma; "conflict industry" formal economics; "payoff perturbations" / game-manipulation literature; a formal principal-agent model of blowback.

### 1.3 Third-party intervention in civil war — effect sizes

**Regan, Patrick M. "Third-party Interventions and the Duration of Intrastate Conflicts." *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 46(1), 2002, pp. 55–73.**
- Result: **"third-party interventions tend to extend expected durations rather than shorten them."** The only aspect of intervention strategy that *reduces* the likelihood of termination in the next month is that it be biased in favour of one side — "neutral interventions are less effective than biased ones."
- Method/sample: hazard analysis; **150 civil conflicts 1945–1999, 101 with outside intervention.**
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. This is the single most directly relevant effect for the paper's premise: outside involvement lengthens war.
- URL: https://econpapers.repec.org/article/saejocore/v_3a46_3ay_3a2002_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a55-73.htm · DOI 10.1177/0022002702046001004

**Cunningham, David E. "Veto Players and Civil War Duration." *American Journal of Political Science* 50(4), 2006, pp. 875–892.**
- Claim: civil wars last longer as the number of veto players — actors whose consent is required for a settlement — increases.
- Method: hazard analysis of civil wars with a constructed veto-player count.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. (Abstract elided by publisher; claim is the paper's standard characterisation — flag that the exact coefficient was not read.)
- DOI: 10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00221.x

**Cunningham, David E., Kristian Skrede Gleditsch & Idean Salehyan. "It Takes Two: A Dyadic Analysis of Civil War Duration and Outcome." *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 53(4), 2009, pp. 570–597.**
- Claim: strong rebels who pose a military challenge produce short wars and concessions; conflicts where rebels seem weak but can operate in the periphery are prolonged; conflicts are shorter when insurgents have alternative political means.
- Method: dyadic survival analysis of civil war duration and outcome, new data on non-state actors.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT.
- **IMPORTANT — CORRIGENDUM (24 January 2012):** the authors report "an error in the way the data for the original published article was set up for survival analysis in Stata, leading to an incorrect specification of the hazard function in the reported Cox analyses." With corrected setup, "the signs of the coefficients in general do not match with the revised data," significance tests change, and the authors state of one published conclusion that "This statement is no longer correct, since strong central command is now significant." Corrected tables: N = 426 both models.
- URL: https://www.prio.org/publications/39 · http://www.ksgleditsch.com/papers/CorrigendumCunninghametal2009.pdf · DOI 10.1177/0022002709336458
- **Use the corrigendum, not the original, for any duration claim.**

**Fearon, James D. "Why Do Some Civil Wars Last So Much Longer than Others?" *Journal of Peace Research* 41(3), 2004, pp. 275–301.**
- Claim: long wars arise where rebels are weak relative to the government (so neither side can win) — the "sons of the soil" / peripheral insurgency mechanism.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT/INTERPRETATION.
- URL: https://academic.oup.com/jpr/article-abstract/41/3/275/8366607 (abstract not readable this session — flag)

**Regan, Patrick M. "Conditions of Successful Third-Party Intervention in Intrastate Conflicts." *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 40(2), 1996.**
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1177/0022002796040002006. (Exact findings not read — flag.)

### 1.4 Proxy war and cheap war

**Salehyan, Idean. "The Delegation of War to Rebel Organizations." *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 54(3), 2010, pp. 493–515.**
- Claim: delegation lets states "forgo some of the costs of conflict" but costs them "a degree of foreign policy autonomy."
- Method: principal-agent framing; the article is a research **note** with "preliminary evidence" and testable propositions, not a finished empirical test.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT (verbal principal-agent, no game-theoretic model) — **explicitly preliminary; do not cite as a tested finding.**
- DOI: 10.1177/0022002709357890

**Salehyan, Idean, David Siroky & Reed M. Wood. "External Rebel Sponsorship and Civilian Abuse: A Principal-Agent Analysis of Wartime Atrocities." *International Organization* 68(3), 2014, pp. 633–661.**
- Result: foreign state funding reduces rebels' incentives to win hearts and minds because it removes dependence on local resource extraction; democratic principals and states with strong human-rights lobbies restrain abuse more; **multiple state principals increase abuse because "no single state can effectively restrain the organization."**
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT + FORMAL RESULT (empirical test of a principal-agent argument). Sample/dataset details NOT verified this session.
- DOI: 10.1017/S002081831400006X

**Salehyan, Idean, Kristian Skrede Gleditsch & David E. Cunningham. "Explaining External Support for Insurgent Groups." *International Organization* 65(4), 2011.**
- Result: support is more likely for **moderately strong** groups, where transnational constituencies exist, under international rivalry, and when the government itself receives foreign support.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1017/S0020818311000233

**Bapat, Navin A. "Understanding State Sponsorship of Militant Groups." *British Journal of Political Science* 42(1), 2012, pp. 1–29.**
- Claim: sponsorship "may produce moral hazard, because it can empower groups so much that sponsors cannot control them"; "only moderately weak states and major powers are likely to gain coercive power through sponsorship."
- Method: game-theoretic model + data on militant violence 1989–2001.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.1017/S000712341100007X

**Bapat, Navin A. "Transnational Terrorism, US Military Aid, and the Incentive to Misrepresent." *Journal of Peace Research* 48(3), 2011.**
- Claim: US military aid to host states "creates a moral hazard problem" — hosts lose aid once terrorism ends, so they have "little incentive to accelerate the demise of terrorist groups."
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.1177/0022343310394472

**Byman, Daniel, Peter Chalk, Bruce Hoffman, William Rosenau & David Brannan. *Trends in Outside Support for Insurgent Movements.* RAND MR-1405, 2001.**
- Result: "Out of the 74 post–Cold War insurgencies surveyed, state support, we believe, played a major role in initiating, sustaining, bringing to victory, or otherwise assisting 44 of them." Supporters are "primarily motivated by geopolitics rather than ideology"; support is "primarily a local rather than international phenomenon." Safe havens are "essential"; money "has a powerful effect." On control: "external aid can decrease an insurgent movement's freedom of action. Outside patrons, particularly states, typically seek some measure of control in exchange for their investments. State support is often fickle."
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (qualitative survey, 1991–2000, with dataset).
- URL: https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1405.html (RAND blocked automated fetch; text read via mirror)

**Popovic, Milos. "Fragile Proxies: Explaining Rebel Defection Against Their State Sponsors." *Terrorism and Political Violence* 29(5), 2017, pp. 922–942.**
- Result: "A sponsorship is less costly than a direct military intervention, but rebels often defy orders, desert fighting, or turn guns against their sponsors"; as rebel organisations become "less centralized and formalized," they are more likely to defect because weak central leadership "narrow[s] the manipulative capacity of sponsors."
- Method: multilevel logistic models on a novel rebel-sponsor dataset.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **Best verified item both for the "cheap war" premise and for proxies turning on sponsors.**
- DOI: 10.1080/09546553.2015.1092437

**Kinne, Brandon J. et al. "Transnational Networks and Interstate Competition: How Support for Nonstate Actors Increases Conflict between States." *International Organization*, 2026.**
- Result: state support for foreign nonstate actors "increase[s] a government's liability for the actions of sponsored groups, which leads to unintended confrontations and retaliatory actions by affected targets."
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. Caveat: retaliation is by adversaries, not necessarily by the proxy against the sponsor.
- DOI: 10.1017/S0020818326101349

**Waldman, Thomas. *Vicarious Warfare: American Strategy and the Illusion of War on the Cheap.* Policy Press, 2021.**
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION. Closest scholarly treatment of "cheap war"; not a formal model and not economics.
- DOI: 10.1332/policypress/9781529206999.001.0001

**Rauta, Vladimir. "'Proxy War' — A Reconceptualisation." *Civil Wars* 23(1), 2021.** LABEL: INTERPRETATION. DOI 10.1080/13698249.2021.1860578

"Forum: Conflict Delegation in Civil Wars." *International Studies Review* 23(4), 2021.** Bridges "two literatures that have been talking past each other: external support in civil wars and proxy warfare." LABEL: INTERPRETATION. DOI 10.1093/isr/viab053
**Chinchilla, Alexandra. "Formal Theory and Proxy Wars." In *Routledge Handbook of Proxy Wars*, 2023.** LABEL: FORMAL RESULT (survey). Best pointer to the formal-theory literature; content not verified.

### 1.5 Security dilemma as a rent; game manipulation; conflict entrepreneurs

**Jervis, Robert. "Cooperation under the Security Dilemma." *World Politics* 30(2), 1978, pp. 167–214.**
- Claim: whether the dilemma is benign or malign depends on whether the underlying 2×2 is Stag Hunt or Prisoner's Dilemma, and on the offense–defense balance and differentiation.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.2307/2009958
- **But Jervis models the structure that produces the dilemma; no formal model of *deliberately manufacturing* or *selling* the Pareto-inferior equilibrium was found. This is a genuine hole in the literature and is directly relevant to Area 4.**

**Richardson, Lewis F. *Arms and Insecurity.* Boxwood Press, 1960.** Coupled linear ODE arms-race model; stability conditions determine convergence vs escalation. LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. Two-party, not divide-and-conquer.

**Fearon, James D. "Rationalist Explanations for War." *International Organization* 49(3), 1995, pp. 379–414.**
- Claim: three rationalist mechanisms — (i) private information about relative power/resolve plus incentives to misrepresent, (ii) commitment problems from shifting power, (iii) issue indivisibility (which Fearon himself calls the weakest).
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.1017/S0020818300033324 · https://jfearon4.github.io/publications/

**Powell, Robert. "War as a Commitment Problem." *International Organization* 60(1), 2006, pp. 169–203.** Large, rapid power shifts are a sufficient condition for costly conflict. LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.1017/S0020818306060061

**Hirshleifer, Jack. "The Paradox of Power." *Economics and Politics* 3(3), 1991, pp. 177–200.** Under standard contest technology the poorer party has a higher marginal return to fighting. LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0343.1991.tb00046.x
**Grossman, Herschel I. "A General Equilibrium Model of Insurrections." *American Economic Review* 81(4), 1991, pp. 912–921.** LABEL: FORMAL RESULT.
**Skaperdas, Stergios. "Cooperation, Conflict, and Power in the Absence of Property Rights." *APSR* 86(3), 1992, pp. 720–739.** LABEL: FORMAL RESULT.
**Fearon, James D. "Ethnic War as a Commitment Problem." APSA conference paper, 1993/94.** LABEL: FORMAL RESULT — **WEAK: unpublished conference paper.**

**Fearon, James D. & David D. Laitin. "Ethnicity, Insurgency, and Civil War." *APSR* 97(1), 2003, pp. 75–90.**
- Verbatim result: "after controlling for per capita incomes and growth rates, more ethnically or religiously diverse countries have been no more likely to experience significant civil war in this period… The factors that explain which countries have been at risk for civil war are not their ethnic or religious characteristics but rather the conditions that favor insurgency. These include poverty and slow growth… rough terrain, and large populations."
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **This is the central empirical obstacle to a strong "ethnic entrepreneur manufactures conflict" story.** DOI 10.1017/S0003055403000534

**Brubaker, Rogers. "Ethnicity without Groups." *European Journal of Sociology* 43(2), 2002, pp. 163–189.**
- Claim: the "groupism" critique — treating ethnic groups as substantial, internally homogeneous collective actors is a fallacy; "ethnic conflict" is often a framing imposed by participants and analysts.

- **The specific passage criticising elite-manipulation theses was NOT verified. Do not quote it.**
- https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=23561

**Olson, Mancur. "Dictatorship, Democracy, and Development." *APSR* 87(3), 1993.** Roving vs stationary bandit; autocracy as private provision of order. LABEL: MODEL PROJECTION. DOI 10.2307/2938736
**Tilly, Charles. "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime." In *Bringing the State Back In*, Cambridge UP, 1985.** Protection rackets. LABEL: INTERPRETATION.
**Gambetta, Diego. *The Sicilian Mafia: The Business of Private Protection.* Harvard UP, 1993.** LABEL: INTERPRETATION.

### 1.6 Blowback

**Johnson, Chalmers. *Blowback: The Costs and Consequences of American Empire.* Metropolitan/Henry Holt, 2000; *The Sorrows of Empire.* Metropolitan, 2004.**
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION. **Johnson's exact one-sentence definition could not be verified from the primary text.** The popular paraphrase ("unintended consequences of policies kept secret from the American people") is uncited in this dossier. What is verified: Johnson writes that it is "unavoidable that some of these 'secret U.S. government operations and acts in distant lands would come back to haunt us'"; the phrase was "apparently coined by the CIA."

**Holmes, Stephen. "Apocalypse Now?" *The Nation*, 29 October 2007 (review of Johnson's *Nemesis*).**
- Criticism, verbatim: "The book's principal shortcoming… is the inherent slipperiness of the concept of blowback. Johnson's determination to establish that subsequent harms to America are caused by prior American misdeeds often seems fanciful… Such intimations of causality don't quite succumb to the fallacy of post hoc, ergo propter hoc, but they veer perilously close." Also: "the largest problem with blowback is the sheer miscellaneous variety of Johnson's examples," making the grouping "merely rhetorical."
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION (critique). **Venue caveat: *The Nation* is a magazine, not peer-reviewed.** This is nonetheless the most substantive critical engagement with the concept located.
- https://archive.globalpolicy.org/empire/history/2007/1029apocalypse.htm

**The 9/11 Commission Report — what it actually says (printed pp. 55–56), verbatim:**
> "The international environment for Bin Ladin's efforts was ideal. Saudi Arabia and the United States supplied billions of dollars worth of secret assistance to rebel groups in Afghanistan fighting the Soviet occupation. This assistance was funneled through Pakistan: the Pakistani military intelligence service (Inter-Services Intelligence Directorate, or ISID), helped train the rebels and distribute the arms. But Bin Ladin and his comrades had their own sources of support and training, and they received little or no assistance from the United States."

Footnote 23, verbatim: "In his memoir, Ayman al Zawahiri contemptuously rejects the claim that the Arab mujahideen were financed (even 'one penny') or trained by the United States… CIA officials involved in aiding the Afghan resistance regard Bin Ladin and his 'Arab Afghans' as having been militarily insignificant in the war and recall having little to do with him. Gary Schroen interview (Mar. 3, 2003)."

Printed p. 55, verbatim: the Afghan Arabs' funding was "a financial support network that came to be known as the 'Golden Chain,' put together mainly by financiers in Saudi Arabia and the Persian Gulf states. Donations flowed through charities or other nongovernmental organizations."

- **Bottom line, verified:** (a) US aid went to rebel groups and was channelled through Pakistan's ISI/ISID — not to Bin Ladin; (b) Bin Ladin and the Arab Afghans "received little or no assistance from the United States"; (c) the Commission roots their funding in Saudi/Gulf private networks; (d) **the Commission does NOT endorse the blowback causal story** — it roots Bin Ladin's anti-Americanism in specific grievances (US troops in Saudi Arabia, Iraq sanctions, US support for Israel) and in Qutbist ideology, and never uses the word "blowback" or names Operation Cyclone.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (official fact-finding).
- URLs: https://www.9-11commission.gov/report/911Report_Ch2.htm · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_9-11_Commission_Report_(Official_Government_Edition).djvu/485 (endnotes)

- **NOT VERIFIED — do not use:** the commonly cited Operation Cyclone figures (total US aid ~$2–3bn; Saudi dollar-for-dollar matching; ~$630m peak year); the Peter Bergen and Steve Coll critique passages; George Crile's *Charlie Wilson's War*.

### 2.3 Sanctions effectiveness and the "34% success rate"

**Verdict: the "34%" figure does not survive as stated.** It traces to the *second*-edition dataset — Hufbauer, Schott & Elliott, *Economic Sanctions Reconsidered: History and Current Policy*, 2nd ed., Institute for International Economics, 1990: **"of 116 cases of sanctions since 1914, only 34 percent have been successful."** It is **not** a 3rd-edition (2007) figure. PIIE's own summary of the 3rd edition says sanctions **"'succeed' in about one-third of cases overall"** — a loose restatement, not a precise 34%. **Attributing "34%" to the 2007 third edition is imprecise; the correct attribution is "34% of 116 cases, 2nd edition (1990)."** https://www.piie.com/events/sanctions-success-possible-not-common
(Note: the 1990 34%-of-116 figure itself was traced only to a secondary source this session — Bahrami & Parsi, *Boston Review* 2012. Label that specific number **WEAK**; the PIIE "about one-third" formulation is primary.)

The dataset itself, as reported by co-author Elliott in 1997 testimony (before the 3rd edition):
- **115 cases, World War I to 1990; 40 successes, 75 failures = 35% success ratio.** Cases involving the US as sanctioner: 26/78 = 33%. Unilateral US sanctions: 16/55 = 29%; but 1945–70 = 69% and 1970–90 = **13%**. One-third of successes were "at least partially successful" — a deliberately weak success criterion.
- https://www.piie.com/commentary/testimonies/evidence-costs-and-benefits-economic-sanctions

**The critique:**
- **Pape, Robert A. "Why Economic Sanctions Do Not Work." *International Security* 22(2), 1997, pp. 90–136.** Claim: only **5 of the 40 cases HSE coded as successes were correctly designated** — a critique of the coding, not a "5% overall success rate." Pape's stated reason: the majority of HSE's coded successes were in fact settled by the threat or use of military force, which HSE "had not adequately controlled for." **Pape's own text was not readable this session (JSTOR/MIT Press); the "5 of 40" figure comes from a secondary source and is flagged WEAK here.** DOI 10.1162/isec.22.2.90
- **Pape, "Why Economic Sanctions Still Do Not Work." *International Security* 23(1), 1998, pp. 66–77.** DOI 10.1162/isec.23.1.66
- **Elliott, Kimberly Ann. "The Sanctions Glass: Half Full or Completely Empty?" *International Security* 23(1), 1998, pp. 50–65.** HSE co-author's reply: Pape mischaracterises the study's objective — it asked whether sanctions **contributed** to outcomes, not whether they **caused** them. DOI 10.1162/isec.23.1.50
- **Drury, A. Cooper. "Revisiting Economic Sanctions Reconsidered." *Journal of Peace Research* 35(4), 1998.** Reanalysis with ordered logit: the relationships between variables and HSE's recommendations are insignificant, "calling the accuracy and importance into question." LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1177/0022343398035004006
- **Siddiquee, Muhammad & Peter A. G. van Bergeijk. "Reconsidering Economic Sanctions Reconsidered." ISS Working Paper 549, 2012.** ~17% of cases common to the 2nd and 3rd editions were modified/recoded; average success score rises 6.6 → 7.0; the 3rd-edition methodology is "biased in favour of finding positive results." LABEL: MEASUREMENT — **WEAK (working paper), but by named academics.**
- **Drezner, Daniel W. "The Hidden Hand of Economic Coercion." *International Organization* 57(3), 2003.** Game-theoretic selection-effects argument: the observed success rate is understated because most successful coercion ends with threats, not imposed sanctions. LABEL: FORMAL RESULT + MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1017/S0020818303573052
- **Morgan, T. Clifton, Navin Bapat & Valentin Krustev. "The Threat and Imposition of Economic Sanctions, 1971–2000." *Conflict Management and Peace Science* 26(2), 2009, pp. 92–110.** New TIES dataset of 888 threatened and/or implemented cases. LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1177/0738894208097668
- **Bapat & Morgan. "Multilateral Versus Unilateral Sanctions Reconsidered." *ISQ*, 2009.** With TIES data, multilateral sanctions **do** appear more likely to work — contradicting the earlier HSE-based finding. LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-2478.2009.00569.x
- **Bapat, Heinrich, Kobayashi & Morgan. "Determinants of Sanctions Effectiveness." *International Interactions*, 2013.** Institutional involvement and severe costs to the target are robustly related to success at every stage; flags selection effects. LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1080/03050629.2013.751298
- 2025 meta-analysis, *Econometrics* 13(2):16, DOI 10.3390/econometrics13020016 — meta-analysis of 37 studies published 1985–2018. **Authors not retrieved this session — flag.**

**Unverified numbers (do not use):** HSE 3rd-edition sample size (174 vs 204 episodes); the 39.5% unilateral / 54.8% multilateral TIES success rates.

### 2.4 ICJ Nicaragua — a third party actually held to account?

**ICJ, *Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (Nicaragua v. United States of America)*, Judgment of 27 June 1986.** Verified from the Court's own press release 86/8:
- (2) 12–3: rejects the US justification of collective self-defence.
- (3) 12–3: **"the United States of America, by training, arming, equipping, financing and supplying the contra forces or otherwise encouraging, supporting and aiding military and paramilitary activities in and against Nicaragua, has acted… in breach of its obligation under customary international law not to intervene in the affairs of another State."**
- (4) 12–3: breach of the duty not to use force (specific attacks listed, 1983–84). (5) 12–3: breach of sovereignty. (6) 12–3: mining of ports unlawful.
- **(9) 14–1: the Court finds the US "has encouraged the commission by them of acts contrary to general principles of humanitarian law; but does not find a basis for concluding that any such acts which may have been committed are imputable to the United States of America as acts of the United States of America."** ← This is a directly on-point **attribution** holding: the sponsor is liable for what it does, not for what its proxy does.
- (12)–(15): duty to cease and refrain; obligation to make reparation; form and amount reserved to the Court.
- The Court also records that after the 1984 jurisdiction judgment **"the United States decided not to take part in the present phase of the proceedings"** (non-appearance under Article 53).
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (authoritative legal fact-finding) + FORMAL RESULT (legal holding).
- https://icj-cij.org/node/100900

**Enforcement failed:**
- Draft resolution **S/18250 vetoed by the USA, 31 July 1986 (11–1–3)**, meeting S/PV.2704.
- Draft resolution **S/18428 vetoed by the USA, 28 October 1986 (11–1–3)**, meeting S/PV.2718 — the resolution calling for full and immediate compliance with the ICJ judgment.
- https://www.un.org/depts/dhl/resguide/scact1986_table_en.html · https://www.securitycouncilreport.org/un-documents/document/s18428.php

**Both readings, stated fairly:** (a) enforcement *worked declaratorily* — an authoritative finding of state responsibility and real diplomatic isolation; (b) enforcement *failed materially* — no compliance, no reparation ever paid, and the Council blocked by the violator's own veto. Nicaragua is best described as the paradigm case of **judgment without punishment**, and it is the closest thing in the record to a third party being held to account.

### 2.5 Attribution and deniability

**Rid, Thomas & Ben Buchanan. "Attributing Cyber Attacks." *Journal of Strategic Studies* 38(1–2), 2015, pp. 4–37.**
- Claim: attribution "is what states make of it"; the "Q Model" — tactically attribution is an art as well as a science; operationally it is a nuanced process, not black-and-white; **strategically it is a function of what is at stake politically.**
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION. DOI 10.1080/01402390.2014.977382 · https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/attributing-cyber-attacks/

**Lindsay, Jon R. "Tipping the scales: the attribution problem and the feasibility of deterrence against cyberattack." *Journal of Cybersecurity* 1(1), 2015, pp. 53–67.**
- Result: many low-value anonymous attacks, few high-value ones; attribution difficulty and deterrence efficacy are **scale-dependent, not technological constants**; "Deterrence works where it is needed most, yet usually fails everywhere else."
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. Open access. DOI 10.1093/cybsec/tyv003
- **This is the best available formal link from attribution difficulty to punishment probability.**

**Carson, Austin. "Facing Off and Saving Face: Covert Intervention and Escalation Management in the Korean War." *International Organization*, 2015.**
- Claim: secrecy creates "sustainable limits on war"; mutual concealment produces "tacit collusion"; evidence = secretly deployed Soviet pilots.
- LABEL: INTERPRETATION + case evidence. DOI 10.1017/S0020818315000284
**Carson, Austin. *Secret Wars: Covert Conflict in International Politics.* Princeton UP, 2018.**
**Poznansky, Michael. *In the Shadow of International Law: Secrecy and Regime Change in the Postwar World.* Oxford UP, 2020.** Existence verified; **abstract not retrievable — findings UNVERIFIED.**

**EMPTY/THIN: no widely cited formal principal-agent model of deniability → punishment probability was verified.**

### 2.6 Coalition coordination costs and the number of parties

**Olson & Zeckhauser 1966** — see 2.1. Disproportionate burden-sharing. FORMAL RESULT + MEASUREMENT.

**Wolford, Scott, Joshua Landry & Kevin Galambos. "Collective Deterrence." *International Organization* 80(3), 2026.**
- Model: one state may challenge a status quo defended by a great power and an arbitrary number of partners; defender war costs are private information. Results: great-power intra-coalition dominance determines which cooperative failure (free riding vs uncertainty) weakens deterrence; increasing the great power's war payoffs increases both deterrence and partner free riding; **the extent of great-power dominance determines whether a larger or more capable coalition enhances or weakens deterrence, and "size and capabilities work in opposite directions"**; increasing uncertainty over the great power's war costs can decrease *or* increase deterrence depending on the prior strength of its commitment.
- LABEL: FORMAL RESULT. This is the single best verified formal result on number-of-parties and deterrence.
- https://econpapers.repec.org/article/cupintorg/v_3a80_3ay_3a2026_3ai_3a3_3ap_3a_5f5.htm

**Aambø, Øyvind. "Collective deterrence as a classification problem: Voting rules, deterrence credibility, and escalation risk." arXiv:2604.08482.**
- Claim: the social-choice function for retaliation creates a trade-off between credible deterrence and escalation risk; recast as a binary classification problem; ROC curves computed for a coalition of **four** members.
- LABEL: MODEL PROJECTION. **Preprint, not peer-reviewed.**

**Kim, Palfrey & Zeidel. "Crisis Bargaining with Collective Decision Making." NBER WP 33392, 2025.**
- Experiment varying payoffs, group size, and voting rules; behaviour inconsistent with Perfect Bayesian Equilibrium, broadly consistent with team equilibrium.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (experiment). **Non-peer-reviewed working paper.** DOI 10.3386/w33392

**Morey, Daniel. "Too Many Cooks: Coalitions and the Outcome of Interstate War." Working paper, SSRN 1643950.**
- Result: MEASUREMENT over interstate war outcomes 1816–1997 — coalition members win "almost three times" as often; as command and control centralises, odds of victory "greatly increase."
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **WEAK (working paper; peer-review status unverified).** NOTE: **this cuts against** the "more allies = weaker" claim.

**Walt, Stephen M. *The Origins of Alliances.* Cornell UP, 1987.** States balance against threats, not just power; balancing alliances have natural credibility. LABEL: INTERPRETATION. (Verified only via a summary site — WEAK.)
**Weitsman, Patricia. *Dangerous Alliances: Proponents of Peace, Weapons of War.* Stanford UP, 2004.** Existence verified; content not verified.
**Morrow, James D. "Alliances and Asymmetry." *AJPS* 35(4), 1991.** Existence verified; abstract not retrieved.

**EMPTY: no quantified estimate of coordination cost as a function of the number of parties was found.** Searches run: coalition formation and number-of-players effects on success probability under unanimity/supermajority; coordination costs rising with party count; alliance size and credibility; Plümper/Plister; Weitsman; Morrow; Riker's size principle and its critique. **The coordination-cost claim must be reported as asserted, not measured.** The closest formal item is Wolford et al. 2026 (number of partners, but deterrence not enforcement); the closest experimental item is NBER w33392.

## AREA 3 — Who profits, and how much

### 3.1 The economic effect of a war on third countries

**Federle, Jonathan, Andre Meier, Gernot J. Müller, Willi Mutschler & Moritz Schularick. "The Price of War." Kiel Working Paper 2262, February 2024. DOI 10.1257/aer.20241355 (published in the *American Economic Review*).**
- Result: on a new dataset spanning **150 years and more than 60 countries**, war on a country's territory typically leads to an output decline of **30%** and a **15 percentage point** increase in inflation. Output in neighbouring countries falls by **more than 10% over 5 years**, and inflation rises by **5 percentage points** on average — "irrespective of their participation in the war." Negative spillovers decline with geographic distance and increase in the degree of trade integration with the war site. **"For very distant countries, output spillovers can turn positive so that wars create winners and losers in the international economy."**
- Method: historical macro-panel estimation, rationalised in an international business-cycle model calibrated to the data; the mechanism is that capital destruction at the war site plus falling productivity reduces trade with nearby economies, generating an endogenous supply-side contraction abroad.
- **The methodology DOES distinguish winners from losers**: the sign of the third-country effect is explicitly conditional on geographic distance and trade integration, and turns positive at sufficient distance. It does **not**, however, identify *which named countries or sectors* gain, and it does not decompose gains by actor.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (historical panel) + MODEL PROJECTION (the AER model and any counterfactual).
- https://www.kielinstitut.de/publications/the-price-of-war-17477/

**Kiel Institute, "Economic Fallout: The Price of War," 14 February 2024 — the Ukraine scenario.**
- Numbers: Ukraine ~**USD 120bn** output loss and **>USD 950bn** capital stock loss by 2026; non-belligerent third countries ~**USD 250bn** output loss, of which the **EU ~USD 70bn** and Germany **USD 15–20bn**. Taiwan scenario ~**USD 2.2tn** global output loss within five years; Iran scenario up to **USD 1.7tn**.
- **CRITICAL LABEL: this is a scenario projection applying historical averages to a hypothetical, not a measurement of the actual war.** The Kiel release itself says "The calculations are based on the costs of 'typical' interstate wars in the past. Depending on the duration and intensity of the war, more or less severe scenarios are conceivable" and, of the calculator, "the calculations can only be regarded as a ballpark rather than a precise estimate."
- LABEL: MODEL PROJECTION.
- https://www.kielinstitut.de/publications/news/economic-fallout-the-price-of-war/

**Dirks, Maximilian W. "When two quarrel, the third rejoices: Windfall FDI and the early winners of the Russian-Ukrainian war." Ruhr Economic Papers 1161, 2025.**
- Result: using the invasion as a quasi-exogenous shock, a sharp decline in Western FDI into Russia and Russia-leaning countries alongside a significant increase in cross-border investment into bloc-free nations — **Brazil, India, Mexico and Saudi Arabia among the primary beneficiaries**; a Bayesian VAR estimates these windfall FDI **raised GDP in recipient countries by up to 2%**.
- Method: difference-in-differences on Orbis Crossborder investment data + Bayesian VAR.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **Working paper, not yet peer-reviewed.**
- DOI 10.4419/96973345 · https://ideas.repec.org/p/zbw/rwirep/325493.html

**Hayakawa, Kazunobu & Satoru Kumagai. "Trade Effects of the Russia–Ukraine Conflict: Can Neutral Countries Really Fish in Troubled Waters?" IDE Discussion Paper 944, 2024; published in *Scottish Journal of Political Economy* 73(3), 2026.**
- Result: examining exports from **32 neutral countries**, on average neutral countries **significantly increased** their exports to Russia after the invasion, **but** the increase was smaller in neutral countries with a greater presence of Western MNEs, and exports to Russia even **decreased** from neutral countries with the highest Western-MNE presence.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **This cuts against a simple "neutrals gain" story** and is the right citation for the disagreement.
- https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/jetdpaper/dpaper944.htm

### 3.2 War profiteering — the defence sector

**Kaja, Fatjon & Kevin Foster. "The market dynamics of military conflict: financial returns and strategic considerations in the global arms industry." *Defence and Peace Economics*, 2025.**
- Result (event study on the largest publicly traded arms companies from the SIPRI Top 100): after Russia's February 2022 invasion, arms-industry stocks recorded an average of **10 percentage points of cumulative abnormal returns in the first week**, which did not dissipate; **one month after the invasion, European defence firms exhibited mean CARs exceeding 20%**; more than **70% of European arms companies in the sample had higher returns than the median non-European ones**. By contrast, following the October 2023 Hamas attack on Israel, the same companies recorded **no statistically significant abnormal returns at all**. Some firms (e.g. Boeing) had raw cumulative returns of **−1% to −7.5%** during the Ukraine event.
- Interpretation offered: markets respond to **strategic policy changes**, not to war itself — Germany's EUR 100bn fund, NATO 2% pledges, Finnish/Swedish accession. Israel–Hamas produced no comparable realignment (Israel is the world's 15th-largest military spender, ~1% of global defence expenditure).
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **Use the peer-reviewed article, not the blog post.** DOI 10.1080/10242694.2025.2582088 · https://safe-frankfurt.de/news-latest/safe-finance-blog/details/what-two-conflicts-reveal-about-investor-expectations-in-the-global-arms-industry.html (author-written summary)

**Key caution for the paper:** this literature measures *shareholder returns conditional on policy response*, not "who profits from war." There is **no measured estimate of a spoiler's profit** in this literature. The defence-sector event-study result is the closest measurable analogue and it shows that war alone is an unreliable predictor.

### 3.3 Arms transfers and conflict

**Moore, Matthew. "Selling to Both Sides: The Effects of Major Conventional Weapons Transfers on Civil War Severity and Duration." *International Interactions* 38(3), 2012, pp. 325–347.**
- Result: **rebel acquisition of major conventional weapons from international sources leads to conflict escalation and deadlier conflicts; state importation of major conventional weapons is associated with longer conflicts.**
- Method: recipient-based approach to arms transfers, tested on civil war severity and duration.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. DOI 10.1080/03050629.2012.676511
- https://econpapers.repec.org/article/tafginixx/v_3a38_3ay_3a2012_3ai_3a3_3ap_3a325-347.htm

**Pamp, Oliver, Lukas Rudolph, Paul Thurner, Andreas Mehltretter & Simon Primus. "The Build-up of Coercive Capacities: Arms Imports and the Outbreak of Violent Intrastate Conflicts." *Journal of Peace Research*, 2018.**
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT. **The exact effect size was not extracted this session — flag as a lead requiring the published PDF.**
- https://www.gsi.uni-muenchen.de/lehreinheiten/le_policy_analysis/dokumente/papersdata/pamp_et_al_2017_draft.pdf

**SIPRI Arms Transfers Database — the measurement caveat that must travel with any citation.**
- SIPRI measures **Trend Indicator Value (TIV)**, "based on the known unit production costs of a core set of weapons," intended "to represent the transfer of military resources rather than the financial value of the transfer."
- SIPRI states explicitly: **"SIPRI TIV figures do not represent sales prices for arms transfers. They should therefore not be directly compared with gross domestic product (GDP), military expenditure, sales values or the financial value of export licences in an attempt to measure the economic burden of arms imports or the economic benefits of exports."**
- Coverage: **major conventional weapons only.** The database **excludes small arms and light weapons** (other than MANPADs/guided ATGMs), trucks, artillery under 100 mm, ammunition, support equipment and services. Used weapons are valued at 40% of new; refurbished at 66%.
- LABEL: MEASUREMENT (with an explicit non-money caveat).
- https://www.sipri.org/databases/armstransfers/sources-and-methods
- **Consequence for the paper: TIV cannot be used to estimate a spoiler's revenue from arms sales. There is no SIPRI series that measures profit.**

### 3.4 Are sponsors later targeted by their own proxies?

**Popovic 2017** — see 1.4. The best verified quantitative result: rebels "turn guns against their sponsors," and decentralised rebel organisations defect more. LABEL: MEASUREMENT.
**Kinne et al. 2026** — sponsor liability for proxy actions generates "unintended confrontations and retaliatory actions by affected targets." LABEL: MEASUREMENT.
**Byman et al. 2001** — qualitative case: Kabila "turned against his former patrons… in an act of astounding treachery, he allied himself with the same Hutu militias that were formerly supported by Mobutu and against whom he had been fighting." LABEL: MEASUREMENT/INTERPRETATION.
**The 9/11 Commission Report** — see 1.6. **The Commission does not endorse the "our proxy came back for us" causal story, and states Bin Ladin and the Arab Afghans "received little or no assistance from the United States."** Anyone asserting the mujahideen/al-Qaeda blowback chain must engage this text directly.

**Sparse:** no dedicated quantitative study of "sponsor later attacked by its own proxy" as a dependent variable. The category is **thin, not empty**.

## AREA 4 — What cannot be parameterised from published evidence

For each quantity a model of a profit-seeking spoiler would need, and whether it is **merely unmeasured** (data could in principle exist) or **unconstructible in principle** (the quantity has no observable counterpart, or is not identified even with full data).

1. **The spoiler's revenue from prolonging a conflict.** Unmeasured, and arguably unconstructible. There is no published series measuring what any third party earned *because* a war continued. The Kiel work measures third-country GDP effects of the war site's destruction, and the defence-event-study work measures share returns conditional on policy response — neither attributes revenue to duration. Constructible only via a structural model with an explicit counterfactual, which is a MODEL PROJECTION, not a measurement.

2. **The causal effect of a spoiler's action on conflict duration.** Unmeasured, and identification is unconstructible in principle for the general case. Duration models (Regan 2002; Cunningham 2006; CGS 2009 + corrigendum) estimate the association between *observed* third-party involvement and duration; they do not isolate intentional prolongation, and involvement is endogenous to the conflict. No instrument or natural experiment for "spoiling" exists in the published record.

3. **The price of a manufactured security dilemma.** Unmeasured and unconstructible in principle as stated. No formal model of deliberately manufacturing the dilemma was found, and no market in which such a good is transacted has been observed. This is the paper's central construct and it has no empirical counterpart in the literature.

4. **A spoiler's cost of deniability.** Unmeasured. No published estimate of the resource cost of maintaining plausible deniability; the attribution literature (Rid & Buchanan 2015; Lindsay 2015) is conceptual/formal and does not price deniability.

5. **The probability that a covert sponsor is punished, conditional on attribution.** Unmeasured. Lindsay 2015 gives a formal scale-dependent result for cyberattack deterrence; no estimate exists for the general case, and covert operations are by construction censored from the data.

6. **Coordination cost as a function of the number of opposing parties.** Unmeasured. **This is the most important gap in Area 2.** The literature asserts that coordination costs rise with the number of parties; no quantified estimate was found. The closest items are Wolford et al. 2026 (formal, deterrence not enforcement) and NBER w33392 (experiment, small n). Unmeasured rather than unconstructible — a laboratory or historical dataset could in principle supply it.

7. **The success rate of economic sanctions.** Measured, but **the measurement is contested and the widely cited figure is imprecise.** The honest range from the sources verified here: HSE 2nd edition 35% of 115 cases (40 successes) on a deliberately weak "partial success" criterion; Pape's re-coding dispute; Drury's reanalysis finding the relationships insignificant; Siddiquee & van Bergeijk finding ~17% of cases recoded between editions and the 3rd-edition methodology "biased in favour of finding positive results." **A model should carry a range, not a point estimate, and should say so.**

8. **The profit of an arms producer attributable to a specific conflict.** Partially measured (Kaja & Foster 2025 CARs) but **unconstructible as "profit from the war"** because the measured quantity is abnormal *shareholder return* conditional on policy shifts, and SIPRI TIV — the standard arms-transfer measure — explicitly is not a money value and cannot be used to compute revenue or profit.

9. **The spoiler's own counterfactual cost of the war it fomented.** Unconstructible in principle. Requires the counterfactual world in which the spoiler did not act; no published method identifies it.

10. **The spoiler's identity and intent.** Unconstructible in principle from observational conflict data. The spoiler problem literature (Stedman 1997; Nilsson & Kovacs 2011; Zahar) is explicit that spoilers are contested as a category, and Fearon & Laitin 2003 undercuts the assumption that conflict is manufactured by identifiable entrepreneurs. **A model may assume a spoiler; it cannot estimate one from the published record.**

11. **The number of veto players / effective number of parties in a given conflict, as a parameter the spoiler can influence.** Measured as a correlate of duration (Cunningham 2006) but **not measured as a spoiler's policy instrument.** Unmeasured.

12. **Third-country gains from others' wars, disaggregated to named beneficiaries.** Partially measured: positive spillovers at sufficient distance (Federle et al. 2024, directional not named); windfall FDI to Brazil, India, Mexico, Saudi Arabia worth up to 2% of GDP (Dirks 2025, working paper); neutral-country export gains that shrink and even reverse with Western-MNE presence (Hayakawa & Kumagai 2026). **No published estimate identifies a state that profited from a war by design.** Unmeasured, plausibly unconstructible for the general case.

**Summary of the four categories:**
- **Merely unmeasured:** the spoiler's revenue; coordination cost vs number of parties; probability of punishment conditional on attribution; veto-player count as a spoiler instrument.
- **Measured but contested:** sanctions success rate — report as a range with the coding dispute attached.
- **Measurable only through a structural model (therefore MODEL PROJECTION, not measurement):** third-country GDP effects of a hypothetical war; the counterfactual duration effect.
- **Unconstructible in principle:** the price of a manufactured security dilemma; the spoiler's identity and intent from observational data; the counterfactual cost to the spoiler of the war it fomented; profit attributable to a specific conflict from SIPRI TIV.
