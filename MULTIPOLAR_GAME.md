# The Fourth Class: Consumers, Guardians, and the Multipolar Game

*A critical extension of [`Platos_Tripartite_Order_and_Happiness.md`](Platos_Tripartite_Order_and_Happiness.md).
That chapter used Plato's three classes to map the dimensions of personal
happiness. This chapter turns the same framework outward, toward the world
economic and political system itself, and asks a question directly: what
happens when the classes are inverted — when reason is subordinated rather
than ruling, when a fourth class Plato never named comes to dominate
circulation without producing, and when the instrument every class now
reaches for to compete is the same one, AI?*

---

## 1. The inversion: philosophers conscripted, not ruling

Plato's ideal city requires reason to govern spirit and appetite — the
philosopher-king, trained in wisdom, ruling the guardians and producers for
the good of the whole. The diagnosis is that the present order has inverted
this: scientists and philosophers are not absent from power, they are
*present but subordinated* — conscripted into the guardian function
(technical experts serving defense, security, and surveillance
establishments) or the producer function (researchers whose work is directed
by corporate return rather than by the pursuit of truth for its own sake),
rather than governing in their own right.

This is close to Eisenhower's own warning in his 1961 farewell address, which
cautioned that the growing fusion of military and industrial power risked
capturing the very scientific and academic establishment meant to inform
public policy independently — a fusion he named directly and worried
specifically about the "scientific-technological elite" losing its
independence to exactly this kind of conscription. The concern is not new;
what has changed is its scale and its instrument.

---

## 2. The Fourth Class: Consumers

Plato's tripartite soul does not have a missing category for what I am pointing
at, and it is worth being precise about why it needs one. Plato's producers —
farmers, craftsmen, merchants — are still, in his account, *producers*: they
make or grow something real. This new class is different in
kind: people and institutions positioned in the circulation of value —
between production and final use — who consume media, goods, and wealth in
large volume while contributing comparatively little direct production to
society. Financial traders, layers of logistics and distribution
intermediaries, and administrative middlemen across the chain from factory to
final consumption are the paradigm cases.

This is not merely a moral complaint; it has real academic grounding as a
distinct economic category:

- **Rent-seeking theory** (Gordon Tullock, 1967; Anne Krueger, "The Political
  Economy of the Rent-Seeking Society," 1974) formally distinguishes
  *profit-seeking* (creating value and being rewarded for it) from
  *rent-seeking* (capturing a share of value already created by others,
  through position rather than production) — precisely the distinction
  between Plato's producer and my proposed fourth class.
- **David Graeber's "Bullshit Jobs"** (2018) argues, from extensive survey
  data, that a large share of jobs in finance, administration, and
  middle-management are perceived as pointless even by the people holding
  them, and function primarily to circulate or extract value rather than
  create it — a direct empirical echo of my description.
- **Thomas Philippon's research on the finance industry** ("Has the U.S.
  Finance Industry Become Less Efficient?", 2015) found that the cost of
  financial intermediation in the United States has *not* meaningfully
  fallen despite decades of technological improvement that should have
  reduced it — evidence that a persistently large share of financial-sector
  revenue reflects rent extraction rather than a productive service whose
  price should fall with efficiency, closely matching "financial
  trader, middleman" framing.

A fourth class, added to Plato's three, is therefore a defensible extension,
not just a rhetorical addition:

| Class | Soul part (extended) | Function | Modern examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Producers | Appetite | Make or grow real goods | Manufacturing, agriculture, craft, engineering |
| Guardians | Spirit | Defend and enforce order | Military, security services, policing |
| Rulers | Reason | Govern through wisdom | Philosopher-kings (largely displaced today, per Section 1) |
| **Consumers** *(proposed)* | Hedonism | Circulate, intermediate, and extract from value already produced | Financial trading, logistics/distribution middlemen, rentier platforms |

## 3. Rulers who have become Guardians: securing resources, not deliberating wisdom

My observation that "rulers are nowadays 'Guardians'" describes something
international relations theory has a name for: **securitization** (Barry
Buzan & Ole Wæver's Copenhagen School, developed through the 1990s), the
process by which political issues — energy, technology, trade, even climate —
are reframed as existential security threats, which justifies extraordinary,
guardian-class measures (export controls, military posture, surveillance) in
place of ordinary deliberative governance. Once an issue is securitized,
governance shifts from Plato's reason-class register (weighing competing
goods through argument) to the guardian-class register (defending against a
threat), regardless of whether the underlying issue actually required that
framing.

This connects directly to my point about resource security: a ruling
system organized substantially around **securing natural resources and
consumption capacity** rather than around wisdom-based deliberation is, on
Plato's own terms, guardians ruling in reason's place — an inversion of his
intended hierarchy, not a variation within it. Michael Klare's *Resource
Wars* (2001) and the broader "resource curse" literature (Michael Ross,
Paul Collier) document this pattern empirically: control over oil, minerals,
and — increasingly, per
`FASHION_FORCED_OBSOLENCE_AND_TECHNO_FEUDALISM.md` §7 — the water, energy,
and critical minerals (rare earths, lithium, cobalt) that AI infrastructure
now depends on, has repeatedly been a proximate cause of interstate and
intrastate conflict. A ruling order whose central organizing task is
resource security is, by construction, structured around conflict readiness
rather than around Plato's reason-class task of adjudicating the good of the
whole city.

## 4. From unipolar to multipolar: the game theory of everyone optimizing at once

The post-Cold War unipolar order, led by a single dominant power with no peer
competitor, has been giving way through the 2010s and 2020s to a genuinely
multipolar system, with the United States, China, and a growing set of
"middle powers" — the EU, India, Gulf states, and others — each pursuing
independent strategic optimization rather than aligning within a single
hegemonic order, and 2026 analysis explicitly frames AI capability itself as
a primary axis of this great-power competition, alongside more traditional
military and resource measures.

This is worth analyzing in genuinely game-theoretic terms, because the
academic literature is split on whether multipolarity is more or less stable
than what preceded it, and an honest treatment should present both sides
rather than assert one:

- **The pessimistic (realist) case**, associated with John Mearsheimer and
  the broader structural-realist tradition, holds that multipolar systems are
  historically *less* stable than bipolar ones: more independent great powers
  means more possible conflict dyads, more opportunities for miscalculation,
  and weaker deterrence than the comparatively simple, well-understood
  balance of a two-power standoff. On this view, the transition itself is the
  dangerous part — power transitions between a rising and an established
  power have historically been associated with conflict often enough that
  Graham Allison termed it the "Thucydides Trap" (*Destined for War*, 2017).
- **The more optimistic case**, represented in the 2026 analysis above by
  economist Diane Coyle's argument, holds that AI competition specifically
  need not be zero-sum: middle powers can make independent strategic choices
  rather than being forced into a single camp, and the underlying technology
  is bound up with global supply chains, collaborative research, and
  cross-border data flows in ways that traditional military rivalry was not —
  a structural feature that could support cooperative, positive-sum outcomes
  rather than pure zero-sum competition.

The core game-theoretic mechanism underlying the pessimistic case is the
**security dilemma** (John Herz, 1950): a state (or firm, or bloc) increasing
its own security or capability — more compute, more resource control, more
military posture — reduces the *relative* security of others, who then
respond in kind, producing an arms-race dynamic that can leave every party
worse off than a cooperative equilibrium would have, even though each
individual step was locally rational. This is structurally the same
Pareto-inferior Nash equilibrium already formalized in
`THEORY_OF_SPARING.md` §7d, scaled up from a producer-consumer pricing game
to a civilizational one: no single actor can unilaterally de-escalate without
being disadvantaged relative to competitors who do not.

## 5. Everyone is optimizing — and now with the same tool

Optimization is no longer confined to states, banks or insurances. Workers
optimizing their own labor supply (the subject of this entire project),
hyperscalers optimizing datacenter capex (`FASHION_FORCED_OBSOLENCE_AND_TECHNO_FEUDALISM.md`
§5–7), governments optimizing resource security (Section 3 above), and supply
chains optimizing logistics and pricing (Section 6 below) are all now running
some form of AI-driven forecasting and strategic modeling to anticipate and
respond to each other's moves — an *n*-player, simultaneous, AI-augmented
optimization problem without a clean, closed-form solution. Thomas Schelling's
work on strategic bargaining (*The Strategy of Conflict*, 1960) already
showed, well before AI existed, that outcomes in this kind of interdependent
strategic setting depend heavily on credible commitment and each party's
model of the others' likely responses — AI changes the speed and apparent
precision of that modeling, not its fundamentally interdependent structure.

The honest answer to "where is the equilibrium and when will it be achieved"
draws on both game theory and history: multipolar systems have occasionally
found temporarily stable, negotiated equilibria — the Concert of Europe
(1815–roughly 1848–1914) is the standard historical example, a genuine
multipolar great-power balance that held for decades through deliberate
diplomatic coordination — but that same equilibrium eventually broke down
into the First World War once the underlying security-dilemma pressures
outpaced the diplomatic mechanisms managing them. There is no general
theorem guaranteeing a multipolar system converges to a stable equilibrium at
all, let alone a date by which it will. The AI dimension makes this
particular transition historically unprecedented rather than a repeat of the
Concert of Europe or the Cold War, which is itself a reason for humility
about predicting its resolution.

## 6. Algorithms deciding life, work, and price: who actually captures the gain

Following closing examples deserve direct, concrete treatment rather than
abstraction, because each now has a documented real-world case attached.

**Cheaper products.** The clearest current case is the opposite of the
promise: the U.S. Department of Justice sued RealPage, a rental-pricing
algorithm provider, alleging its software let landlords effectively
coordinate rent increases by sharing non-public competitor pricing data
through the algorithm — "replacing competition with coordination," in the
DOJ's own phrase. RealPage settled with the DOJ in November 2025 without
admitting liability, agreeing to restrict the data its algorithm can use;
parallel class-action settlements exceeding $140 million with various
landlord defendants were preliminarily approved around the same time; and
New York separately amended its antitrust law in October 2025 to explicitly
prohibit algorithmic rent-setting tools. This is a directly on-point,
still-unfolding example of the fourth class (a software intermediary
sitting between property owners and renters) using an algorithm to extract
rather than pass through efficiency gains — the opposite of the "cheaper
products" a consumer might expect from automated optimization.

**Finding a job.** Already discussed in `HAPPINESS_OR_FEAR_WORK_LIFE.md`
§5 — algorithmic management on gig platforms substitutes continuous,
precise, scaled disciplining pressure for the more limited, negotiable
pressure a human manager could apply, industrializing rather than easing the
extraction dynamic already present in the labor market.

**Life and death.** Autonomous weapons systems and algorithmic triage in
healthcare and criminal justice (the COMPAS recidivism-scoring controversy
being the most widely documented criminal-justice case) place real
consequential decisions inside systems optimizing a narrow, specified
objective function rather than exercising Plato's reason-class wisdom, which
by its nature weighs competing goods contextually. This is arguably a new
category of power that does not fit cleanly into any of the four classes
above: not a producer, not a guardian, not a consumer, and certainly not a
reasoning ruler in Plato's sense — a tool wielded *by* whichever class
controls it (predominantly, at present, the guardian and consumer classes),
rather than an independent class of its own.

---

## 7. So who wins?

The honest answer has to resist the temptation of a single, satisfying
villain, and should present the genuinely live possibilities rather than
assert one:

1. **The consumer/cloudalist class captures the gains, at least in the near
   term.** This is the throughline of `HAPPINESS_OR_FEAR_WORK_LIFE.md`
   §5 and `FASHION_FORCED_OBSOLENCE_AND_TECHNO_FEUDALISM.md` §5–8: platform
   owners positioned in circulation, not production, are best placed to
   capture the surplus from AI-driven efficiency, exactly as the RealPage case
   illustrates concretely.
2. **No one wins, in the classical sense — the security-dilemma dynamic
   produces a costly, unstable equilibrium that leaves most participants
   worse off than a cooperative alternative would have**, consistent with the
   Pareto-inferior Nash equilibrium structure already used in
   `Theory_of_Sparing.md` §7d and Section 4 above. An AI-accelerated arms race
   between states, and a simultaneous AI-accelerated rent-extraction race
   between platforms, can both be individually rational and collectively
   self-defeating at the same time.
3. **The consumer class's own instrument may erode the consumer class's own
   position.** This is the ironic possibility worth naming: algorithmic
   trading has already displaced large numbers of human financial
   intermediaries; further AI-driven disintermediation could, over time,
   compress the middleman value Section 2 describes, echoing the Jevons-
   paradox-style unintended consequence already identified in
   `FASHION_FORCED_OBSOLENCE_AND_TECHNO_FEUDALISM.md` §8c — the tool
   built to extract more efficiently may end up automating the extractors
   themselves.
4. **Genuine uncertainty is the most defensible position.** The presence of
   AI as a simultaneously economic and military strategic variable, layered
   on top of a multipolar transition with no settled historical precedent,
   means confident prediction is not epistemically warranted. This is not
   evasion — Section 5's Concert-of-Europe example shows multipolar
   equilibria genuinely can hold for decades, and can also genuinely
   collapse; the honest position is that both outcomes remain live.

## 8. Where reason would need to return, on Plato's own terms

Plato's account of political decay (*Republic*, Book VIII) describes a
sequence: a just, reason-governed city degrades first into timocracy
(honor/guardian-driven), then oligarchy (wealth/producer-driven), then
democracy (appetite pulled in many directions), then tyranny — each stage
further from reason's governance than the last. Paul Kennedy's *The Rise and
Fall of the Great Powers* (1987) offers a strikingly similar empirical
pattern for real historical hegemons: "imperial overstretch," where a
dominant power's military and security commitments (guardian-class spending)
grow to outpace the productive economic base (producer-class capacity)
sustaining them, is a repeated cause of hegemonic decline across centuries
of history — a modern, empirical echo of Plato's decay sequence rather than
a coincidence.

This document series is not positioned to resolve world politics, and should
not pretend to. What can be said on Plato's own terms, consistently with
`Platos_Tripartite_Order_and_Happiness.md` §6's observation that this
project's own habit of self-critique is itself a reason-class exercise: a
system in which guardians secure resources and consumers extract rent while
reason is conscripted into serving both, rather than governing either, is —
on Plato's own definition of justice as each part properly performing its
function under reason's integration — an *unjust* order, structurally, not
merely an unequal one. Whether that diagnosis translates into any actionable
remedy at civilizational scale is a genuinely open question this chapter
does not claim to answer; naming the structure accurately is the more modest
and more honest goal.

## 9. Summary

- My perspective is that philosophers today are conscripted into guardian or
  producer service rather than ruling maps onto a real, documented concern —
  Eisenhower's 1961 warning about the fusion of military, industrial, and
  scientific power was substantially the same observation.
- A fourth class — Consumers — is a defensible extension of Plato's
  framework: unlike Plato's producers, this class circulates and intermediates
  value rather than creating it, and has real academic grounding in
  rent-seeking theory (Tullock, Krueger), Graeber's "Bullshit Jobs," and
  Philippon's finding that financial intermediation costs have not fallen
  despite decades of technological improvement.
- "Rulers as Guardians" maps onto securitization theory (Buzan & Wæver):
  governance reframed around resource and security threats rather than
  reasoned deliberation, with resource-driven conflict (Klare's *Resource
  Wars*, the resource-curse literature) as a predictable consequence.
- The unipolar-to-multipolar transition is real and current, and AI is now a
  primary axis of great-power competition alongside traditional military and
  resource measures — the academic literature is genuinely split between
  Mearsheimer's pessimistic realist view (multipolarity is less stable, more
  conflict-prone) and a more optimistic view (AI competition need not be
  zero-sum), and this chapter presents both rather than asserting one.
- The security dilemma (Herz, 1950) is the core game-theoretic mechanism
  behind "everyone optimizing at once," structurally identical to the
  Pareto-inferior Nash equilibrium already formalized in
  `Theory_of_Sparing.md` §7d, now operating at civilizational scale.
- Algorithmic decisions over pricing (the RealPage case), employment
  (already covered in `Fear_HAPPINESS_Work_Life_Balance.md` §5), and life
  and death (autonomous weapons, algorithmic triage) increasingly sit outside
  all four classes — tools wielded by whichever class controls them, not an
  independent locus of reasoned judgment.
- "Who wins" has no single confident answer: the consumer/cloudalist class is
  best positioned near-term, but a security-dilemma-style mutual escalation
  could leave most participants worse off, and AI-driven disintermediation
  could eventually erode the consumer class's own advantage — genuine
  uncertainty, not evasion, is the most defensible position given the
  historically unprecedented combination of multipolar transition and AI
  competition.
- Plato's own account of political decay, and Paul Kennedy's empirical
  "imperial overstretch" thesis, both describe guardian-class overextension
  outpacing producer-class capacity as a recurring cause of decline — a
  structural, not merely moral, argument for why an order where reason does
  not govern is unstable on its own terms, independent of any judgment about
  which current power bloc is right.
